We'd been gone three and a half weeks. Today's inspection took three and a half hours. Somehow that felt about right — a long stretch away deserved a long day making sure everything was still on track.

Suits on, smoker lit, replacement gear stocked up, and we finally got back up on the roof. Three hives to check, and honestly, a little bit of nerves going in. A lot can happen to a hive in over three weeks with nobody watching.

## The First: steady as ever

We started here, same routine as always — checked for fresh eggs, looked for any signs of swarming. Everything looked normal. This hive has been quietly doing its job since it stopped needing the sugar water feeder months ago, and today was no exception. Mite test came back at 2 out of 300, which is a clean, healthy number. No concerns, no surprises. Sometimes the best inspection is a boring one.

## The nuc: the best hive we've got

The nuc might be running away with the title of strongest colony at this point. We checked the honey super we'd added to see if the bees were actually drawing it out and using it, and they are — the hive is, in Asher's words, blowing up. Strong population, actively working the super, clearly thriving. The mite test came back at zero out of 300. Not low. Zero. For a hive that had a mite problem earlier in the season and needed chemical strips, that's about the best possible outcome. Whatever we did right with this one, it worked.

## The OG: she made it

This is the one we'd been waiting weeks to check properly.

If you've been following along, you know the story. Back on day 75 we found two queen cells in the OG and left them to develop. On day 85 and day 88 we deliberately stayed out of the hive to give a virgin queen room to do her orientation and mating flights undisturbed. By day 98, we checked and found nothing — no eggs, no sign she was laying, and a colony that had been broodless for weeks and losing numbers every day. We gave it until Sunday. If nothing had changed, we were going to buy a replacement queen.

Then we left for vacation.

Today, walking back into that hive not knowing what we'd find, we checked for eggs and swarming signs like always. And there they were — eggs, and not just eggs, whole frames of brood already capped. The OG made her own queen, and that queen made it. She mated, came home, and got to work, we just weren't there to see the moment it happened. Mite count was 1 out of 300, which means on top of everything else, she's heading a healthy colony.

It's hard to describe the relief of that. This was the hive we almost gave up on. The one where doing nothing felt like the harder choice than doing something. Turns out that was the right call — she just needed more time than we expected, and we're glad we didn't rush it.

## The first honey

And then, the part we'd been waiting all season for.

Today we pulled our first frame of honey. One frame — not even a full box — came out to about 4 pounds. Standing there holding it, that's a strange thing to process. Every hive check, every feeder swap, every mite test, all of it leads to this one dense, heavy frame.

The process starts with a special knife for uncapping — you slice off the thin wax caps the bees seal each cell with once the honey inside is fully cured. Underneath that cap is the honey itself. Today we scraped the cappings off and dumped everything into a bowl. Eventually we'll use a proper extractor to spin the honey out of the comb more efficiently, but for a first frame, doing it by hand felt right.

What we haven't figured out yet is how to cleanly separate the wax from the honey once it's all mixed together in the bowl. That's the next problem to solve, and we'll write about it once we've worked it out.

## Coming home to good news

You never really know what you'll find after being away that long. Today we found a hive we'd almost written off instead thriving, two other colonies in great shape, and our very first honey. Three and a half hours on the roof, and it might have been the best inspection of the season.
